    What Are Meta Tags?

    Small pieces of HTML that tell search engines and browsers about your page.

    Meta tags live in the <head> of a page and aren't visible on the page itself. The two that matter most for SEO are the title tag — the blue headline in Google results and the text in the browser tab — and the meta description, which Google often uses as the snippet under the title.

    Good titles and descriptions don't directly boost rankings much, but they strongly affect click-through rate: a clear, specific, benefit-driven snippet gets more clicks than a vague one.

    The robots tag controls whether a page can be indexed and whether its links are followed; canonical tells Google which URL is the main version when the same content is reachable at several addresses (with tracking parameters, http/https, trailing slashes).

    Google truncates titles by pixel width (about 580 px), not character count — “WWW” takes far more room than “iii”. This tool measures your title in pixels the same way, so the preview shows where it will be cut.

    🏷 TAG GUIDE

    Meta Tags Explained

    What each generated tag does and when to use it.

    T

    <title>

    Required on every page. Unique, descriptive, 50–60 characters (under ~580 px). Main keyword near the start, brand at the end.

    D

    meta name="description"

    A unique 120–160 character summary. Google may rewrite it, but a good one is used most of the time and improves clicks.

    R

    meta name="robots"

    index, follow is the default. Use noindex for thank-you pages, internal search results and staging sites. max-image-preview:large allows big image previews in Discover.

    C

    link rel="canonical"

    The absolute, preferred URL of the page. Prevents duplicate-content issues from URL parameters and alternate addresses.

    V

    charset & viewport

    Every modern page needs <meta charset="UTF-8"> (first in the head) and the responsive viewport tag for mobile-friendly rendering.

    K

    meta name="keywords"

    Ignored by Google since 2009 and by Bing as a ranking signal. Harmless, but optional — it can reveal your keyword strategy to competitors.
